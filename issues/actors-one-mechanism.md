# Actors, signing and forks as one mechanism
> Summary: Design study wanted by Tess: an account is a special kind of thing (members by signing chain, no grants on it, its own fork and merge rules, an account root standing in for it). Direction: one composable rule for who sees who acted, forks handled as admin disputes are, and a hard look at what hiding nodes costs. Nothing designed yet.
> Priority: normal
> Tags: tess-call, perm, crypto, keys, spec

## What is special today
An account's members are its nodes, kept by its signing chain (spec/permissions.md §5.4-§5.5, spec/crypto.md §9, notes/engine-validity-epochs.md), not by role grants: no edge write targets it, nothing derives from its epochs (things minted with it as admin are independent at birth), and the account root stands in for it wherever a thing is needed. So an account cannot hold a role on another account's account. It has its own fork rules (sides by first revocation seen; merges of concurrent rotations) beside the admin-dispute rules and the role-key rule (forks stand).

## Direction (Tess)
- **One nestable rule for who sees who acted**: those inside an actor (holders of some role on it) see which member acted; those outside see the actor acting. The same for nodes in an account, accounts in an org, and any other nesting.
- **Hiding is not welded to the actor**: an account may act through an org and stay visible as itself, or show its node. Showing more must be easy. The existing "acting for" header field is part of this and gets rethought with it. Sketch (Claude): a commit carries its signatures from the node up, each layer sealed to the next layer's insiders or left clear, the outermost being the public signer; above it, plain grants. One choice per write: where hiding stops; default the account.
- **Attribution**: the API gives the public signer and what it acted for, favouring neither; the grants above are a separate question with possibly several answers; whose face shows is app policy.
- **Accounts on accounts**: an actor being its own only admin is one arrangement of roles, never a kind of actor or its definition.
- **Forks**: two members removing each other has no right answer inside osis, whatever the members are: both sides go on, others pick a side by a heuristic, warn, and may switch (as admin disputes and account sides do today). Never a required merge, never a global winner. Account forks, admin disputes and key forks should be one mechanism; say what the account's merge of concurrent rotations is for and whether forks standing covers it.
- **Simplify account signing by design**: it grew from requirements set before their cost was known. Hiding nodes inside an account is where the cost comes from: a removed member's writes cannot be told from the others' by who signed, only by epoch, so the honest line names every commit it stands by (thing lists, listings, list trees, issues/rotation-names-grow.md). Weigh three points: hidden as now; nodes visible as ordinary members with ordinary removal (valid, dispreferred by Tess); something between (a pseudonym per node per thing, or per session). State what each costs in bytes per commit, in machinery, and what a reader learns.

## Removal lists (one mechanism wanted)
- Both removals rest on one idea: timestamps are claims, so the remover says what it received of the removed party, and the rest falls. A demotion below write on a holder N lists X's commits on every thing N gave write on (spec/permissions.md §5.1 Frontier), as bare commit IDs in the removal commit, which every reader of any of those things holds as a certificate; an account's rotation lists per thing in a blinded tree (§5.5). The first has the need the tree answers (readers of one thing, lists about others) and no answer, and a size limit (`MAX_LIST_BYTES`: some removals cannot be written). To check what a reader of one thing learns from a frontier today, then give both one form.
- Frontiers list commit IDs because nodes share one signing key (notes/crdt.md, Second dot form). A writer signing as itself (a node, or a pseudonym of one) is sequential, so a removal could name one tip per writer: but checking a commit against a tip needs something kept per commit.
- **Constraint (Tess): nothing may be kept per commit for good.** With a sensible configuration a node writes constantly for 20 years without filling its disk. Today a forgotten commit is not listed; the remover's own cover snapshot carries its live writes: vouching for a state where the history is gone. Any new form keeps that property.
- **Pseudonyms (Tess: open to it, value depends on grain).** Grains, coarse to fine: per node; per node per thing (readers of a thing tell an account's devices apart there, never across things); per session; per commit (today: nothing linkable, everything listed). A session is one run of a node (spec/crdt.md §2.1), already visible to readers, and exists to keep CRDT dots and snapshot frontiers compact; commit form is the session-per-commit case and pays 32 B per commit named. Sessions are not reliably rarer than commits (each CLI call, each short-lived process is one), so nothing may rest on their being few; check what already does.

## Tess's position on hiding nodes
- Node anonymity was chosen before its removal cost was known. A pseudonym per node per thing is acceptable, and so are plainly visible nodes if that is significantly simpler.
- Claude's reading, to check in the study: visible nodes is much the simpler. Every commit is signed by a node's own key, an account is an independent thing whose admins are its nodes, a device removal is an ordinary removal, a device fight is an admin dispute, and the signing chain, epochs, custody, merges, sides and lists go. Readers of anything an account writes then learn its device count, which device made each change, and when devices come and go, linkable across all they read. A pseudonym per node per thing needs something to vouch that the pseudonym is a member without naming the node: a key the members share, so a reduced signing chain and its revocation stay.
- The same cost follows hiding wherever it is offered (an org's anonymous voice), so the nestable hiding rule in Direction is decided with this: offered generally and paid for where used, or not at all.
- **Sessions**: 32 B per commit would be acceptable, so sessions are an optimization, to be kept only if worth their complexity. Measure what they save: beyond the one ID, runs of counters do not continue across commits in commit form, so a sequence written a commit per element pays a source per element in its state. With visible writers, ask whether a session is still a separate idea or the writer with durable counters. Part of this study; needs nothing from Tess.
- **If the design moves off node anonymity, keep a note of it** (Tess; a deliberate exception to notes holding no past states): what the design was, what it cost in machinery and bytes, the choices and trade-offs here, so a later look at node anonymity starts ahead. Distil it from notes/engine-validity-epochs.md and the spec sections before they go.

## Why a signer is a device, never finer (Claude; Tess asked)
- **A signing identity is the unit of removal**: it should be exactly as fine as what can be lost, stolen or removed apart from the rest, which is one key store. Coarser (members sharing a key) and a removal cannot tell the members' writes apart: the cost of hiding. Finer (a key per session or commit from one store) and stealing the store steals them all, so a removal must cover every identity that store made or can make, which needs them linked to it: by a visible root (then that is the identity) or by a shared key (hiding again). Nothing is gained either way.
- Per-identity costs, paid at adding and removing, fine for devices and absurd per commit: a grant on the account, its secrets boxed to the newcomer's KEM key (over 1 KB a role), its key card and grant travelling as certificates under every sync ID it writes in; a box at each later rotation of the account; at removal a removal commit, a rotation and a frontier. Against that, each commit loses the sealed inner signature and epoch (about 127 B).
- At the large end (a fleet of thousands under one account) rotation and certificates grow with the roster: nest accounts instead; check thin clients' minted actors against this.
- **What readers learn** (Tess: only the devices that wrote the thing): in principle a reader of a thing needs the grant, and any removal, of each device that wrote there, nothing of the others. The certificate rules as built hand over more (spec/requirements.md: "the roster of every actor holding a role on it"); the design keeps it to the writers.

## The plain shared-key sketch (Tess) and where it grows
Every device holds the account's private key and signs with it, nothing tying a commit to a device; on a theft a device signs a new key with the old and boxes it to the others; readers treat it as "old key removed, new key added"; a thief doing the same is a fork. Sound, and the outline of what is built. Its cost is inside "treated as a removal": a removal says what the remover received of the removed writer, and the removed writer is the whole account at every key the stolen device held. So the removal vouches for all the account's history while that device was a member, on every thing it writes, split so a reader of one thing gets only its part (lists, the blinded tree); an honest device offline at the time has its unsent writes fall until it vouches for them itself (listings, addenda); two honest devices rotating at once need joining (merges). With visible devices only the removed device's writes are in question and the others' never are. What the sealed inner signature adds over the bare sketch is to be established in the study.

## Hiding as a shared key under the ordinary rules (Tess; the lead candidate to test)
The sketch needs no mechanism of its own if the account is an ordinary independent thing A and the shared key K is a signer that is A's admin: swapping K for K1 is an ordinary removal and grant on A signed by K, travelling as A's role commits do wherever A holds a role; a thief's own swap is an ordinary admin dispute; an offline device's writes are saved by an ordinary addendum from it under K1; two honest devices swapping at once leave A with two admins K1 and K2, both held by both, and nothing to merge. Visible devices are then the same design with one key per device, so an account (or an org) picks hidden or visible by how many hold each key, each option paying its own cost: no hiding mechanism exists at all.
- What it asks of the ordinary removal, owed anyway: lists a reader of one thing gets only its part of, no size at which a removal cannot be written, and a removed writer's forgotten history vouched as state.
- What hidden then costs, as properties to state: a device removal puts the whole account's history at every key the device held in question, on everything it writes (bytes and work in proportion); honest devices' unsent writes fall until they come back and vouch; insiders cannot tell which device wrote unless something like today's sealed inner signature is added.
- To establish: what today's signing chain, epochs, custody, merges and sides do that this does not (each is either a requirement this misses or a duplicate of an ordinary rule), and how devices learn each other's keys to box a new key without a roster readers can see.

## Plan (Tess)
- Two modes over the ordinary rules: **visible** (each device signs with its own key; probably the default) and **hidden** (devices share a key; hidden from everyone, their own account included). Each should cost little beyond what other features need anyway, both together far less than today's account system. The choice applies at any level of a chain of holders.
- Which is the default, whether the other is opt-in, a toggle, or unexposed to end users, follows from their trade-offs once known. Possibly only one is built and the other kept as a design.
- A key swap must not read as one person leaving and another joining: the account stays the identity, its keys are members. Decide how a write says so (the signer acting for the account, as the header's acting-for field does today) with the attribution rule in Direction.
- Most of what is missing is bugs and limits in the ordinary removal (above), to be fixed whatever is decided.
- Claude's suggestion: study and model both, build visible first (it needs nothing hidden does not), and keep hidden a checked design so nothing built rules it out. Changes spec/requirements.md ("never which node made a change").

## What should fall out (Tess): identities managed through another
Not features to build: a check on the design, and UI over ordinary features later. Each is an account thing with some arrangement of member keys.
- **Unlinked** (Tess's "sock puppet"; name open: persona, alias): an account whose one member is a shared key all the owner's devices hold. Nothing shows the owner's account, or that it differs from any single-device account. The key travels between the owner's devices as private data of the owner's account.
- **Shared or nested**: the same key also held by another account's devices; or a member that is itself such an account.
- **Provably linked**: an account whose admin is the owner's account by an ordinary grant, the chain visible.
- Why it matters: where privacy counts is often not where edits are many or their history audited, so choosing per identity softens the hidden mode's costs.
- Limits for now (Tess: well-known problems, to be mitigated when privacy features are built, not part of this design): the same node serving both identities and both profiles naming the same addresses; every identity a stolen device held swapping keys at once; edits' timing.

## Signers, actors, profiles and accounts (working model; Tess and Claude)
- **Signer**: a key. A device's own (the node's key: its node root and settings stay its local things, signed by it alone), a key several devices share (hidden mode), a link's. A device holds its own and any shared keys it was given. A commit has exactly one signer: one signature.
- **Actor is a part played in a commit, not a kind of thing** (Tess): a commit has a chain of actors, starting with its signer, passing through the things it acts through, and ending at the things it acts on, which are not its actors. No thing "is" or "is not" an actor. As "holder" is a thing with a role on something, an actor is a thing on a commit's path.
- **Explicit path** (Tess: preferred over today's search for any chain of grants that gives the right, a source of subtle problems and cost): the commit states its path and is judged along it alone, each hop checked as Acting is today (spec/permissions.md §5.2). Removing a person from an org then touches what they wrote through it and nothing they wrote by a direct grant, and the evaluation walks one path (issues/perm-eval-cost.md).
- **What the path lists**: independent things only. A dependent thing has exactly one way up, its mint admins to its independent, fixed by its record, so those hops follow from the thing written and are never stated. The path runs from the signer to a thing holding a role on the written thing's independent (or to that independent itself).
- **Several things in one commit: a full path per thing written** (Tess). Multi-thing commits exist for atomicity, not to save bytes, and are neither most commits nor large. Things sharing an independent share a path anyway (dependent hops are implied). Possible optimization, only if it stays simple: one path stated for the commit, used by every thing that states none of its own; a path of its own goes beside that thing's parts, so a reader of one thing learns nothing of a path used only on another. Rejected: one path per commit with the last actor holding a direct role on everything written (a write across two authorities is refused or split, and apps would have to understand why); a set of actors from which paths are derived (the search again).
- **Profile** (Tess): the public face of an independent thing, a name and a picture, what a write is shown as. Any actor on a path may have one; which one an app shows is its policy. A news outlet's public identity is a thing with members and a profile and nothing else.
  - A separate thing, generally not on the path (Tess, Claude): roles are per thing, and those who may see the face must not read the actor's inside.
  - **The actor names its profile** (Tess): a reference on the actor, so holding the actor brings the profile in, and a reader that cannot get it sees a broken reference, never silently nothing. One profile per actor: several, or one a reader may not know of, are downsides (another face is another identity).
  - Rejected: proving whose profile it is from the profile's side. By "minted with the actor and written through it": that leans on things being minted empty, which may change, and on the mint admin differing from other admins, which the API must never show (issues/behaviour-depends-on-own-keys.md). By "the actor is its admin": anyone can mint a thing, fill it and hand its admin to the actor.
  - To design (Claude's sketch): outsiders do not read the actor, so the reference has to sit in what they do hold of it to check its hops, its public material: chain certificates (spec/permissions.md §4) already bring every reader of a commit the current `keys.0` commits of each actor on its author's chain, and the actor's `keys.0` already names its inbox there (`Engine::inbox_thing`). Carrying a ticket, the reference would also let whoever can see the actor fetch and read the profile, so a face is seen by exactly those who see the actor act. The dial list that sits on today's profile is for contacts only and would move.
- **Account**: more than a profile. An arrangement apps engage with from the inside: a profile, an inbox, private things its devices share (a home, the device list), members that are signers. A workspace or realm may have a like arrangement. A convention over ordinary things (a schema), technically possible to set up otherwise; nothing says it is a person. With the account an ordinary independent thing, its private things can be its dependents, so ask whether the account root is still a separate thing.
- An unlinked identity is an account whose member is a shared key; a linked one has no signer of its own, only the owner's account as admin, signed by the owner's devices.
- The header's `author` and one-level `actor` fields become the signer and the path.

## How a reader gets what it needs to judge a path (to redesign; Tess: today's will not last)
- Today (spec/permissions.md §4): a commit's author's chain certificates are copied into every sync ID carrying a body of it, by whoever logs it there: records, the grants (cut down to their grant deltas) between the things on **any** path from the author to the thing written, those things' current `keys.0` commits, and the same for each grant's author. Sealed under the sync ID's blob key, which opens by the read secret of the sync ID's thing: a reader of the thing whose sync ID carries the commit reads them; a node keeping it unread stores them closed. The engine judges from every role commit it holds, however it came (osis-perm: components of things joined by edges, authors and records, memoized per thing); a header names only its author and one actor.
- Costs: certificate copies that never shrink (issues/certificate-copy-never-shrinks.md; a copy of the same parts is one blob wrapped under each sync ID and fetched once, spec/sync-judgement.md §1, but record and list blobs are a fresh encryption per sync ID), a cut-down copy still showing its commit's whole header (every thing and aspect it touched), and a certificate set that follows every possible path.
- **Goal (Tess): what a reader holds to judge is a function of the data, never of what was sent before.** Her proposal: a sync unit includes what verifies every actor that could write anything in it, so a commit arriving finds its evidence already there, with one actor's data stored once however many units name it.
- Claude's view: adopt it as the direction. It replaces a mechanism (writers logging their chains where they write, closed under authors, tracked per sync ID) with the one that exists (what a sync unit holds), a commit never waits for evidence that was not sent (today: pending until its author's chain arrives, spec/permissions.md §4), and a removal up a path reaches every reader by the same rule. Blobs are shared across sync IDs (one blob, a wrap and stamp per sync ID, spec/sync-judgement.md §1), so an actor's role data in many units is stored and fetched once. To settle:
  - **Readers must open the same blob.** Role parts are sealed under their thing's roles secret; a reader of a thing an actor writes does not hold it, which is why evidence travels today as copies re-encrypted per sync ID by someone who can read it. For the shared blob to serve, a grant has to hand the holder's roles secret down to the readers of what it is granted on (they may learn the roster, spec/requirements.md).
  - **Work per roster change**: an entry under every sync ID the actor can write in (153 B each), as for any thing in many sync units; heavy for an actor holding roles on very many things. The alternative is the actor's role data under its own sync ID only, which readers of what it writes follow: one entry per change.
  - **Size**: a thing shared with a large holder carries its whole roster, and with visible devices every member's devices. Accepted as the cost of a large holder (usage rule: nest rosters), or evidence only for the hops held commits name, which brings back fetching on arrival.
- With explicit paths the evidence a commit needs is its named hops and nothing else. Design the transport for that, together with where the profile reference sits and how a removal up the path reaches readers who judge by what they hold.
